Linear Functions: Gradient and Intercept

A linear function is really just a rule for a straight line, and every straight line is completely described by two numbers: how steep it is, and where it crosses the y-axis. Once you can read those two numbers off an equation, you can picture the line in your head without plotting a single point.

We write it like this:

\[ y = mx + c \]

Here \(m\) is the gradient — how much \(y\) goes up for every step \(x\) takes to the right. And \(c\) is the y-intercept — the value of \(y\) when \(x=0\), i.e. where the line crosses the vertical axis.

Think of gradient as a rate: if \(m=3\), every time \(x\) increases by 1, \(y\) increases by 3. If \(m\) is negative, the line goes downhill as you move right. This is exactly the same idea you'll meet again later as "gradient of a curve" in calculus — you're building the intuition for that right now.

Worked example — find the equation of a line

01A line passes through \((0, 4)\) and \((2, 10)\). Find its equation.
02Gradient: how much \(y\) changes divided by how much \(x\) changes: \( m = \dfrac{10-4}{2-0} = \dfrac{6}{2} = 3 \)
03The point \((0,4)\) is exactly where \(x=0\) — so the intercept is \( c = 4 \)
04Equation: \( y = 3x + 4 \)

Notice we didn't need a formula to "find c" — we just used the point where the line crosses the axis directly. That's usually the fastest way, when you're given it.

→ gradient = rise over run
c is just "where x = 0" ✓
this idea comes back in calculus!

Practice

1

Find the gradient and y-intercept of \( y = -2x + 7 \).

✓ Gradient \(m=-2\) (line slopes downhill), y-intercept \(c=7\) (crosses the axis at \(y=7\)).

2

A line passes through \((0,-1)\) and \((3,5)\). Find its equation.

✓ \( m = \dfrac{5-(-1)}{3-0} = 2 \), and since \((0,-1)\) gives \(c=-1\): \( y = 2x - 1 \).

3

Two lines have gradients 4 and \(\tfrac14\). What do you notice, and what might that mean geometrically?

✓ The gradients are reciprocals of each other. That's a clue, not a coincidence — reciprocal gradients (with opposite sign) show up later when we study perpendicular lines.

Differentiation from First Principles

Let's start with a question you already know the answer to informally: how steep is a curve at one exact point? A straight line has one gradient everywhere. A curve doesn't — its steepness keeps changing. So we need a way to "zoom in" on a single point until the curve looks straight, and measure the gradient there.

Here's the idea. Pick a point on the curve at \(x\), and a second point a little further along at \(x+h\). Join them with a straight line — a chord. Its gradient is:

\[ \text{gradient of chord} = \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \]

Now shrink \(h\) towards zero. The second point slides back towards the first, the chord rotates, and in the limit it becomes the tangent — the exact gradient at that one point. That's the whole idea behind the definition:

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \]

This is what "differentiating from first principles" means — no shortcut rules, just this limit, done directly.

Worked example — differentiate \(f(x) = x^2\) from first principles

01Set up the definition: \( f'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \)
02Substitute: \( f(x+h) = (x+h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2 \)
03So \( f(x+h)-f(x) = 2xh + h^2 \)
04Divide by \(h\): \( \dfrac{2xh+h^2}{h} = 2x + h \)
05Let \(h \to 0\): \( f'(x) = 2x \)

Notice every step is legal algebra until the very last one — we only let \(h\to 0\) after the division has already cancelled the awkward \(h\) in the denominator. That order matters, and examiners will check you've shown it.

→ this is the only bit examiners actually mark closely
nice — this cancels ✓
don't skip this step in an exam!

Practice

1

Differentiate \( f(x) = 3x^2 \) from first principles.

✓ \( f(x+h)-f(x) = 3(x+h)^2 - 3x^2 = 6xh+3h^2 \), divide by \(h\): \(6x+3h\), let \(h\to0\): \( f'(x) = 6x \).

2

Differentiate \( f(x) = x^2 + 4x \) from first principles.

✓ Expand \(f(x+h)\), subtract, divide by \(h\) to get \(2x+4+h\), then let \(h\to0\): \( f'(x) = 2x+4 \).

3

(HL) Show from first principles that the derivative of \( f(x) = x^3 \) is \( 3x^2 \).

✓ Expand \((x+h)^3 = x^3+3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3\). Subtract \(x^3\), divide by \(h\): \(3x^2+3xh+h^2\). Let \(h\to0\): the last two terms vanish, leaving \(3x^2\).

Newton's Laws: Force, Mass, and Acceleration

Here's a question worth sitting with: if nothing pushes or pulls an object, what does it do? Most people's gut instinct is "it slows down and stops." But that's only true because of friction. Out in space, with nothing touching it, an object that's moving just... keeps moving, forever, in a straight line. That's Newton's First Law: an object stays at rest, or keeps moving at constant velocity, unless a force acts on it.

So force isn't what keeps things moving — it's what changes how they're moving. That's Newton's Second Law, and it's one of the most useful equations you'll ever learn:

\[ F = ma \]

Force equals mass times acceleration. Push harder (\(F\) up) and something accelerates faster. Push the same force on something heavier (\(m\) up) and it accelerates slower. That trade-off is worth feeling in your gut, not just memorizing: try pushing an empty shopping cart, then a full one, with the same effort — the full one barely speeds up.

Worked example — pushing a box

01A 10 kg box is pushed with a force of 25 N across a frictionless floor. Find its acceleration.
02Rearrange: \( a = \dfrac{F}{m} \)
03Substitute: \( a = \dfrac{25}{10} = 2.5\ \text{m/s}^2 \)
04So every second, the box's speed increases by 2.5 m/s.

Keep units consistent — force in newtons, mass in kilograms — and the acceleration comes out in m/s² automatically. That's not a coincidence; the newton is defined so that this works out cleanly.

→ no force = no change in motion
heavier = harder to accelerate ✓
keep units in kg, N, m/s²

Practice

1

A 5 kg object accelerates at 3 m/s². What force is acting on it?

✓ \( F = ma = 5 \times 3 = 15\ \text{N} \).

2

A 40 N force acts on an object, giving it an acceleration of 8 m/s². Find its mass.

✓ \( m = \dfrac{F}{a} = \dfrac{40}{8} = 5\ \text{kg} \).

3

Why does a fully-loaded truck take longer to stop than an empty one, if the brakes apply the same braking force?

✓ Same force, bigger mass, means smaller acceleration (\(a = F/m\)) — so it takes longer to slow down. This is exactly why braking distance rules depend on vehicle load.

Circular Motion

Here's something that trips people up at first: an object moving at constant speed in a circle is still accelerating. That feels wrong until you remember what acceleration actually means — a change in velocity, and velocity is a vector. Speed can stay fixed while direction keeps changing, and that's enough.

So which way does this acceleration point? Always towards the centre of the circle. That's why we call it centripetal acceleration — "centre-seeking." Its magnitude is:

\[ a = \frac{v^2}{r} \]

where \(v\) is the speed and \(r\) is the radius of the circle. By Newton's second law, this acceleration needs a real, physical force to cause it — the centripetal force:

\[ F = \frac{mv^2}{r} \]

One thing to be very careful about in IB exams: centripetal force is not a new, separate force sitting on your free-body diagram. It's the name for the net force — tension, gravity, friction, the normal force, whatever is actually providing the pull towards the centre. Never draw an extra arrow labelled "centripetal force."

Worked example — a ball on a string

01A 0.20 kg ball is swung in a horizontal circle of radius 0.50 m at 4.0 m/s.
02Centripetal acceleration: \( a = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \dfrac{4.0^2}{0.50} = 32\ \text{m/s}^2 \)
03Centripetal force needed: \( F = ma = 0.20 \times 32 = 6.4\ \text{N} \)
04In this case, that force is the tension in the string — nothing else is pulling it inward.
→ constant speed, changing velocity
common mistake: don't add an extra force arrow!
points inward, always ✓

Practice

1

A car of mass 1200 kg rounds a flat curve of radius 40 m at 15 m/s. Calculate the centripetal force needed.

✓ \( F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = \dfrac{1200 \times 15^2}{40} = 6750\ \text{N} \). On a flat road, this is provided entirely by friction.

2

A satellite orbits Earth at radius \(7.0\times10^6\) m with speed \(7.5\times10^3\) m/s. Find its centripetal acceleration.

✓ \( a = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \dfrac{(7.5\times10^3)^2}{7.0\times10^6} \approx 8.0\ \text{m/s}^2 \) — this is just gravity, pulling it "inward" the whole time.

3

(HL) A pilot performs a vertical circular loop of radius 250 m at 90 m/s. Find the apparent weight the pilot feels at the bottom of the loop, given mass 70 kg.

✓ At the bottom, seat pushes up (\(N\)) and gravity pulls down; net upward force is centripetal: \( N - mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} \). So \( N = mg + \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = 70(9.8) + \dfrac{70\times 90^2}{250} \approx 686 + 2268 = 2954\ \text{N} \) — the pilot feels about 4.3 times their normal weight.
